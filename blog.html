How Kenyan businesses can rank on page one in 2026

The exact technical and content checklist we run for every client, from site speed to Google Business Profile.

Brian ChebetSEO Lead Oct 18, 2026 5 min read Share

On this page

  1. Start with speed, because nothing else gets measured until you do
  2. Write pages that answer one question each
  3. Fill in your Google Business Profile properly
  4. Get reviews, and reply to all of them
  5. Earn links from places that actually exist in Kenya
  6. Measure enquiries, not rankings

Most Kenyan businesses that fail to rank are not being beaten by better websites. They are being beaten by websites that load faster, answer the question more directly, and have told Google plainly who they are and where they trade. None of that requires a big budget. It requires doing six unglamorous things in order.

This is the checklist we run on every new client, in the order we run it. Work down it and you will have done more than most of your competitors ever will.

Start with speed, because nothing else gets measured until you do

Google is explicit that page experience affects ranking, and in this market the effect is sharper than the averages suggest. Your customer is on a phone, often on a connection that is fine for WhatsApp and painful for a 4MB homepage image. If the page takes five seconds, a large share of the people you paid to attract have already gone.

The three causes we find, in order of frequency, are almost always the same:

  1. Images uploaded straight from a camera or phone at full resolution. A 3,000 pixel wide photograph displayed in a 600 pixel slot is roughly 25 times more data than needed.
  2. Hosting on a server in Europe or the United States with no caching, so every request makes a round trip.
  3. A page loading four different font families, three tracking scripts and a chat widget before it shows a single word.

Compress every image, serve them at the size they are displayed, move hosting somewhere with a presence closer to your customers, and remove any script you cannot name the purpose of. That one afternoon of work usually moves more than the next six months of keyword research.

Write pages that answer one question each

The most common structural mistake we see is a single Services page listing ten things. It ranks for nothing, because it is specifically about nothing. Google has an entire page to choose from and no idea which query it answers.

Split it. If you fit tyres, repair gearboxes and sell batteries, those are three pages, each with its own heading, its own photographs, its own prices or price ranges, and its own enquiry path. Each one can then rank for the thing it is actually about.

A page that tries to rank for ten things ranks for none of them. A page about one thing can own it.

The test is simple: can you state, in one sentence, the question this page answers? If you cannot, neither can Google.

Fill in your Google Business Profile properly

For any business with a physical location or a service area, this single free profile is worth more than most of the website. It is also where we find the most neglect. The profile that wins is rarely the biggest business; it is the one that has been filled in completely.

  • Pick the most specific primary category available, not the broadest one. 'Hardware store' beats 'Store'.
  • Add every service as a named item, with a short description each.
  • Upload real photographs of the premises, the staff and the stock, and keep adding them monthly.
  • Set accurate hours, including public holidays. Wrong hours generate one-star reviews from people who found you closed.
  • Answer every question in the Q&A section yourself, before someone else answers it wrongly.

Get reviews, and reply to all of them

Reviews affect both whether you appear in the map pack and whether anyone chooses you once you do. The businesses that struggle are almost always the ones waiting for reviews to arrive on their own.

Ask at the moment of delivery, not a week later. One sentence from the person who served them, with the link ready on a card or sent by WhatsApp, converts far better than an email campaign. Then reply to every review, including the good ones, in a sentence or two that sounds like a person rather than a policy.

4.4

The average rating across the Kenyan businesses we work with after six months of asking properly. Most started between 3.6 and 3.9.

Earn links from places that actually exist in Kenya

Forget link-buying schemes. The links that move rankings here are the ordinary ones: your industry association, the sacco you belong to, the supplier who lists their dealers, the local paper that covered your expansion, the school whose sports day you sponsored. Each of those is a real relationship you probably already have, and almost nobody asks for the link.

Make a list of every organisation your business is genuinely connected to. Ask each one whether their site lists members, partners or sponsors. That afternoon of asking will outperform any amount of money spent on directories nobody reads.

Measure enquiries, not rankings

Rankings are a means, not the goal. The number that matters is how many people called, filled in the form or sent a WhatsApp message, and which page they were on when they did. If you cannot answer that, you cannot tell a ranking that made you money from a ranking that made you a graph.

Set up conversion tracking before you start the work, so you have a baseline. Then review monthly: enquiries, where they came from, and what each one cost. Everything else is context.

What to take away

  • Speed first. Compress images and cut unused scripts before touching keywords.
  • One question per page. Split the Services page into the things you actually sell.
  • Complete the Google Business Profile. It is free and it is usually the biggest single win.
  • Ask for reviews at handover, and reply to every one of them.
  • Ask for links you have already earned from associations, suppliers and sponsors.
  • Report enquiries, not positions. Rankings that produce nothing are not progress.

What actually works on TikTok for Kenyan brands

Format, posting rhythm, and the kind of content that gets Kenyan audiences to stop scrolling.

Naomi WanjalaHead of Social Media Oct 14, 2026 3 min read Share

On this page

  1. The first two seconds decide everything
  2. What performs, in order
  3. Film it properly, but film it on a phone
  4. Post four to five times a week, not twelve
  5. The comments are the actual sales channel
  6. What to stop doing

Most businesses arrive on TikTok with a video made for television and wonder why nobody watches it. The platform is not a cheaper billboard. It rewards a specific kind of content, and once you understand what that is, a phone and twenty minutes a day is genuinely enough.

The first two seconds decide everything

There is no warm-up. Nobody is waiting politely for your logo animation to finish. The opening frame has to show either the thing itself or the problem it solves, and the first spoken words have to be the point, not the preamble.

Compare two openings for the same hardware shop. 'Welcome to Sokoni Hardware, your trusted partner since 2009' loses almost everyone. 'This is why your tap keeps dripping' keeps them, because it promises an answer.

Lead with the problem or the product. Never with your own name.

What performs, in order

Across the accounts we run, the pattern is consistent enough to plan around.

The least effective thing is the thing most businesses spend the most money on. A polished brand film is expensive, takes weeks, and reaches a fraction of what a thirty-second clip of your technician explaining a common fault will do on the day you film it.

Film it properly, but film it on a phone

Production value here means legibility, not cinematography. The things that matter:

  • Shoot vertical. Horizontal video is cropped and you lose half the frame.
  • Find light, usually by standing near a doorway. Avoid filming with a bright window behind you.
  • Get close for sound, or use a cheap clip microphone. Bad audio loses more viewers than bad picture.
  • Add captions. A large share of viewing happens with the sound off, in a matatu or an office.
  • Keep it between 20 and 45 seconds unless the subject genuinely needs longer.

Post four to five times a week, not twelve

Frequency matters until it does not. Below about three posts a week, the account never builds momentum. Above five, you are mostly reaching the same people again while burning out whoever has to make the videos.

Four a week, sustained for three months, beats twelve a week for three weeks followed by silence. Pick a rhythm you can keep during a busy season, because the busy season is exactly when most accounts go quiet.

The comments are the actual sales channel

This is the part Kenyan businesses get most wrong. Views are not the product. The product is the conversation that starts underneath the video, and it has a short shelf life.

Someone asking 'how much?' in the comments is a buyer with their hand up. If you answer tomorrow, they have already messaged two competitors. Keep five prepared replies for the questions you get constantly, answer in the comments first so other people can read it, then move the detail to DMs.

68%

of first contact on the accounts we manage now happens in comments or direct messages, not on the website or the phone.

What to stop doing

  • Reposting Instagram content with the watermark still on it. The platform notices and so does the audience.
  • Buying followers. They do not buy, and they drag down the engagement rate that drives reach.
  • Chasing every trend sound regardless of fit. One relevant trend a month is plenty.
  • Hiding the price. If you will not say it, people assume it is bad news and scroll on.

What to take away

  • Open with the problem or the product, never with your brand name.
  • How-to content out-reaches polished brand film by roughly five to one.
  • Vertical, well lit, captioned, 20 to 45 seconds. A phone is enough.
  • Four to five posts a week that you can sustain beats a burst you cannot.
  • Work the comments within the hour. That is where the selling happens.

Getting your business named by ChatGPT and AI Overviews

Assistants now answer before anyone clicks. Here is how to be the business they mention.

Alfred KimaniFounder & Strategy Oct 9, 2026 4 min read Share

On this page

  1. How an assistant decides who to mention
  2. Be consistent about who you are
  3. Write the page that answers the question
  4. Get mentioned somewhere other than your own site
  5. Check whether it is working

A growing share of your buyers now ask an assistant before they ask a search engine. They type a full question, read a paragraph of answer, and often act on it without visiting a single website. If your business is not named in that paragraph, you were never in the running, and no ranking report will tell you.

This is a new discipline, but it is not a mysterious one. Assistants are built on top of what they can read, verify and summarise about you.

How an assistant decides who to mention

Simplifying a complicated process: an assistant is looking for a confident, consistent, checkable answer. It favours businesses that are described the same way in several places, that answer the specific question directly somewhere in writing, and that have some third-party corroboration that they exist and do what they claim.

  • Consistency. The same business name, address and phone number everywhere it appears.
  • Specificity. A page that answers the exact question, rather than a brochure that gestures at it.
  • Corroboration. Mentions somewhere other than your own website: press, directories, associations, reviews.
  • Structure. Clear headings, plain sentences, and the facts stated rather than implied.

Be consistent about who you are

This sounds trivial and it is the most common failure. We routinely find a business listed as four different entities: a trading name on the website, a slightly different one on the Google profile, a third on Facebook, and an old address on a directory nobody has updated since 2019. An assistant trying to summarise that will either hedge or pick someone else.

Write down the exact name, address, phone number and one-sentence description. Then make every single place you appear match it, character for character.

4

different versions of the same business name is the median we find when we audit a new client's online presence.

Write the page that answers the question

Assistants quote specifics. 'We offer comprehensive solutions tailored to your needs' is unquotable. 'A standard three-page business website takes us three to four weeks, including photography and a Google Business Profile setup' can be lifted straight into an answer.

For each question your buyers actually ask, write a page or a clearly headed section that answers it plainly:

  1. State the question as the heading, in the words a person would use.
  2. Answer it in the first two sentences. Do not build up to it.
  3. Give the specifics: numbers, timeframes, what is included, what is not.
  4. Say who it is not for. Assistants and people both trust that.

If a sentence could be said by any of your competitors, it will not be quoted about you.

Get mentioned somewhere other than your own site

Self-description is weak evidence. A mention in a trade publication, an industry association member list, a supplier's dealer page, or a local news story is strong evidence. You do not need dozens. A handful of credible, findable mentions that describe you the same way does more than a hundred directory listings.

Check whether it is working

You can measure this directly, which surprises people. Ask the assistants the questions your buyers ask, in the words they would use, and write down whether you appear.

  • Build a list of ten to fifteen real buyer questions.
  • Ask each one in ChatGPT, Gemini and Google's AI answers, once a month.
  • Record whether you are named, how you are described, and who is named instead.
  • Where a competitor is named, go and read the page the assistant is drawing on.

That last step is the useful one. The page being cited tells you exactly what a good answer looks like for that question.

What to take away

  • Assistants favour consistency. One exact name, address and description everywhere.
  • Write quotable specifics, not comprehensive solutions.
  • Answer the question in the first two sentences, under a heading in the buyer's own words.
  • Get a few credible third-party mentions. Self-description carries little weight.
  • Measure monthly by asking the assistants directly and recording who gets named.

Paid Ads

The Google Ads mistakes burning Nairobi budgets

Five settings we fix in the first week of almost every account we inherit, and what each one was costing.

Dennis OtienoGoogle Ads & Analytics Oct 2, 2026 3 min read Share

On this page

  1. 1. Broad match with no negative keywords
  2. 2. No conversion tracking, so nothing can be judged
  3. 3. Seven campaigns on a budget that supports two
  4. 4. Location targeting set to the wrong option
  5. 5. Sending every advert to the homepage

We take over Google Ads accounts most months, and the same five problems account for most of the waste. None of them are subtle. All of them are visible in the first report you open, and all of them can be fixed in an afternoon.

1. Broad match with no negative keywords

Broad match is Google's default and it is lucrative for Google. Left alone, it will show your advert for searches that merely share a theme with your keyword. A driving school bidding on 'driving lessons' will pay for 'driving licence renewal', 'driving jobs in Nairobi' and 'driving games'.

The fix is not to abandon broad match. It is to read the search terms report every week and add anything irrelevant as a negative keyword. Start with the obvious universal ones: free, cheap, jobs, salary, training, pdf, download, second hand, and the names of countries you do not serve.

31%

of a typical inherited account's spend was going to searches the owner would never have paid for, before we added negatives.

2. No conversion tracking, so nothing can be judged

A startling number of accounts optimise for clicks because clicks are the only thing being counted. Google's algorithms are good, but they can only chase the goal you give them. Give them clicks and they will buy you the cheapest clicks available, which are rarely the ones that buy anything.

Before anything else, make sure these three are tracked as conversions:

  • Form submissions, confirmed by a thank-you page or an event.
  • Phone calls from the advert and from the website, with a minimum duration so misdials do not count.
  • WhatsApp clicks, tagged so you can tell which campaign produced the message.

Once those exist, switch the campaign to optimise for them. Cost per click will often rise. Cost per enquiry usually falls, which is the number that pays wages.

3. Seven campaigns on a budget that supports two

Splitting a small budget across many campaigns starves all of them. Google's bidding needs a certain volume of conversions before it can learn, and a campaign getting four conversions a month never gets there.

Three channels run properly beat seven run thinly, every time we have tested it.

Consolidate. One search campaign covering your best-selling services, with tight ad groups, will almost always outperform six thematic campaigns each receiving pocket money.

4. Location targeting set to the wrong option

Google's default location setting includes people merely showing interest in your area. For a Nairobi business, that can mean paying to advertise to someone in another country researching Kenya.

Change it to presence, so you only reach people actually in the areas you serve. Then check the location report monthly, because the places your enquiries come from are rarely the places you assumed.

5. Sending every advert to the homepage

If someone searches for gearbox repair and lands on a homepage listing eleven services, they have to find their way to the thing they wanted. Many will not. Google also scores the mismatch, so you pay more per click for the privilege.

Send each ad group to the page about that specific thing, with the same words in the heading as in the advert. This single change routinely lifts conversion rates by a third without touching the budget.

What to take away

  • Read the search terms report weekly and add negatives. It is the fastest saving available.
  • Track forms, calls and WhatsApp as conversions before optimising anything.
  • Consolidate campaigns so bidding has enough data to learn from.
  • Set location targeting to presence, not interest.
  • Send each advert to its matching page, not the homepage.

Content

Why bottom-of-funnel content beats blogging for traffic

Ten pages aimed at people ready to buy will out-earn fifty aimed at people who are curious.

Alfred KimaniFounder & Strategy Sep 26, 2026 3 min read Share

On this page

  1. What bottom-of-funnel actually means
  2. Why nobody writes them
  3. How to build them
  4. What to do with the blog instead

Most content marketing advice is written by people selling content marketing. It encourages volume: publish weekly, build an audience, and eventually some of them will buy. For a business with a sales target this quarter, that is an expensive way to find out whether it works.

There is a faster route. Write the small number of pages that people read immediately before they buy, and write them better than anyone else in your market.

What bottom-of-funnel actually means

Someone searching 'what is digital marketing' is curious. Someone searching 'digital marketing agency Nairobi pricing' is shopping. The second person is worth many times the first, there are far fewer of them, and almost nobody writes properly for them.

The pages that serve shoppers are unglamorous and they convert:

  • What it costs, or what shapes the cost if you quote per client.
  • How long it takes, start to finish, with the stages named.
  • What is included and what is not.
  • How you compare to the obvious alternative, written honestly.
  • Who it is not for.
  • What happens on day one after someone says yes.

Ten pages for people ready to buy will out-earn fifty for people who are merely curious.

Why nobody writes them

Because they are uncomfortable. They involve committing to specifics, naming competitors, and admitting what you are not good at. A general blog post about industry trends requires none of that, which is exactly why it persuades nobody.

The discomfort is the moat. Your competitors feel it too, which is why the first business in a market to answer these questions plainly tends to keep the advantage for years.

How to build them

  1. Ask whoever answers the phone to write down every question they are asked before a sale. Two weeks is enough.
  2. Group them. You will find six to ten distinct questions, asked constantly.
  3. Give each one its own page, titled in the customer's words rather than your industry's.
  4. Answer in the first two sentences, then give the specifics underneath.
  5. Link them to each other, and from the main navigation, not just the blog.

What to do with the blog instead

Keep it, but demote it. A blog is useful for depth on things you genuinely know, for the press to quote, and for assistants to cite. It is not a traffic strategy on its own, and publishing weekly to no plan is a tax on whoever has to write it.

One thorough piece a month that you would be happy to be judged on beats four that were filled in to meet a schedule.

  • Write for shoppers, not browsers. Fewer people, far more value.
  • Price, timeline, inclusions, comparisons, and who it is not for are the pages that sell.
  • The discomfort is the advantage. Competitors avoid these pages for the same reason you want to.
  • Source them from the phone, not from keyword tools.
  • Keep the blog, demote it. One good piece a month, not four to fill a calendar.

Data & Analytics

How to tell if your marketing is actually working

Four numbers, one dashboard, and a monthly report your directors will actually read.

Dennis OtienoGoogle Ads & Analytics Sep 19, 2026 3 min read Share

On this page

  1. The four numbers
  2. Tracking them is the hard part, and it is mostly setup
  3. Build one screen, not twelve tabs
  4. The monthly report, in one page

Most marketing reports are long because length feels like value. They open with reach, impressions and follower growth, and somewhere on page six, if at all, they mention how many people got in touch. That order is backwards and it hides whether anything is working.

You need four numbers. Everything else is context for those four.

The four numbers

  1. Enquiries. Calls, forms and messages from people who want to buy. Not traffic, not reach. Enquiries.
  2. Cost per enquiry. Everything you spent that month, divided by the number above.
  3. Source. Which channel produced each enquiry, so you can spend more on what works.
  4. Closed business. How many became customers, and what they were worth.

If you can produce those four every month, you can run a marketing budget sensibly. If you cannot, you are guessing confidently.

Tracking them is the hard part, and it is mostly setup

The numbers are only as good as the plumbing. Before you can report anything honestly, these need to exist:

  • A tracked phone number, or call tracking on the website, so calls are attributed rather than assumed.
  • Form submissions firing a conversion event, confirmed by a thank-you page you can test.
  • WhatsApp links tagged by source, so a message from a TikTok advert is distinguishable from one from Google.
  • A simple record of what closed, even if it lives in a spreadsheet. Especially if it lives in a spreadsheet.
61%

of the small business sites we audit have no working enquiry tracking at all. The form emails an inbox and nothing is recorded.

Build one screen, not twelve tabs

A dashboard that takes effort to read does not get read. Ours fit on one screen: the four numbers, this month against last month, and a short list of which pages and campaigns produced the enquiries.

Looker Studio does this free and connects to Google Ads, Analytics and a Google Sheet, which is enough for most businesses. The point is not the tool. The point is that anyone in the business can glance at it and know whether the month is going well.

If the report needs explaining, it is not a report. It is homework.

The monthly report, in one page

We send clients a single page every month with four sections, in this order:

  1. What happened: the four numbers, with last month beside them.
  2. What we did: the work that was carried out, briefly.
  3. What we learned: one or two things the data changed our mind about.
  4. What is next: what we will do in the coming month, and why.

That is enough for a director to read in three minutes and ask a good question about. Anything longer gets filed unread, and an unread report is the same as no report at all.

What to take away

  • Four numbers: enquiries, cost per enquiry, source, closed business.
  • Fix the plumbing first. Untracked calls and messages make every report fiction.

Websites & CRO

Your website is slow and it is costing you customers

What slow actually costs, where the weight comes from, and the fixes that take an afternoon.

Faith MwikaliWeb Design & Development Sep 12, 2026 3 min read Share

On this page

  1. What a second actually costs
  2. Where the weight actually is
  3. The fixes, in order of return
  4. How to check it honestly

Slow websites do not announce themselves. Nobody emails to say they gave up waiting. The cost is entirely invisible, which is why it goes unfixed for years while the owner wonders why a site that looks fine produces so few enquiries.

Here is what it costs, where the weight comes from, and what to do about it this week.

What a second actually costs

The relationship is steeper than most people expect, and it is steeper here than in markets with better connectivity. Your customer is on a phone, on mobile data, often while doing something else.

A site at five seconds is losing roughly a third of the enquiries a site at one second would get from the same traffic. If you are also paying for that traffic through advertising, you are paying twice: once for the click, and again for the enquiry that never happened.

Where the weight actually is

When we measure a slow Kenyan business site, the weight is almost always in the same four places, in this order.

  1. Images. Usually more than 70 per cent of the page. Photographs uploaded at full camera resolution and scaled down by the browser.
  2. Fonts. Four or five families loaded, when two weights of one family would do.
  3. Scripts. Chat widgets, popup tools, three analytics packages, a cookie banner, and a slider nobody scrolls.
  4. Hosting. A shared server far away with no caching, adding a fixed delay to every single request.
4.3s

the median load time of the Kenyan business sites we audit, measured on a mid-range phone on mobile data.

The fixes, in order of return

Compress and resize every image

Export at the size it will actually be displayed, then compress. A hero image rarely needs to be wider than 1,600 pixels, and a thumbnail never needs to be wider than 400. Modern formats help, but correct sizing does most of the work. This alone commonly halves page weight.

Cut the scripts you cannot justify

Go through every third-party script and ask what it does and who looks at its output. In most audits, at least half cannot be answered, which means they are pure cost. Remove them.

Load fonts sensibly

Pick one family, two weights. Serve them with a display setting that shows text immediately rather than leaving a blank space while the font downloads.

Move hosting closer, and cache

A server with a presence near your customers, plus a caching layer, removes a fixed delay from every request. It is often the cheapest single improvement available and it needs doing once.

Most sites can be made twice as fast in an afternoon, without redesigning anything.

How to check it honestly

Test on the device and connection your customers use, not on office wifi on a laptop. Open the site on a mid-range Android phone on mobile data and count seconds until you can read something. That number is the truth. Tools are useful afterwards for finding out why.

  • Images are usually 70 per cent of the weight. Resize before compressing.

Winning the map pack in your neighbourhood

The three results on the map get most of the calls. Here is how to be one of them.

Brian ChebetSEO Lead Sep 5, 2026 3 min read Share

On this page

  1. Get the categories right
  2. Distance you cannot change, service area you can
  3. Prominence is reviews, photos and activity
  4. Make the phone number work
  5. Measuring it

When someone searches for a service near them, Google shows three businesses on a map before it shows a single ordinary result. Those three take most of the calls. Everyone below is competing for what is left.

Getting into those three is not luck, and it is not about being the biggest. It comes down to relevance, distance and prominence, and you can influence two of the three.

Get the categories right

This is the highest-leverage field in the entire profile and most businesses set it once, badly, and never return. Your primary category should be the most specific one that describes what you mainly do.

  • 'Auto repair shop' and 'Transmission shop' compete for different searches. Pick the one you want to win.
  • Add secondary categories for the other things you genuinely do, but do not pad the list.
  • Check what your best-performing competitor uses. It is public information and it tells you what works in your area.

Distance you cannot change, service area you can

Google favours businesses near the searcher, which is fair and unavoidable. What you can do is set your service area honestly and completely, so you appear for the neighbourhoods you genuinely cover rather than only the one you sit in.

If you have more than one branch, each needs its own profile with its own address, phone number and hours. One profile covering three locations will underperform all three.

Prominence is reviews, photos and activity

This is where the work is, and where most competitors stop.

  1. Reviews, consistently. Twenty reviews gathered steadily over six months beats forty that all arrived in one week, which looks bought.
  2. Reply to every one. A short, human reply, including to the critical ones. Prospective customers read the replies more carefully than the reviews.
  3. Photographs, monthly. The premises, the team, the work in progress, the stock. Profiles with fresh photographs get noticeably more interaction.
  4. Posts. Offers, new stock, holiday hours. It takes two minutes and almost nobody does it.
  5. Questions. Seed the Q&A with the questions you are actually asked, and answer them yourself.
81%

of local searches in the accounts we track end in a phone call rather than a website visit. The profile is doing the selling, not the site.

Make the phone number work

A map pack listing mostly produces calls, not clicks. So the phone needs answering, during the hours you have published, by someone who can actually help.

There is no point winning the map pack if the phone rings out. You have paid for the attention and then wasted it.

If you cannot answer every call, add a WhatsApp number to the profile and say in the description that messages are answered within the hour. A message answered beats a call missed.

Measuring it

The profile has its own insights: how many people called, asked for directions, or clicked through. Record those monthly alongside your other numbers. If calls rise while rankings stay flat, the work is still paying.

Getting covered by the Kenyan press without a PR budget

What editors actually want, how to give it to them, and why your announcement is not a story.

Lucy AchiengClient Service Aug 29, 2026 3 min read Share

On this page

  1. Your news is not news
  2. The data angle is the most available one
  3. How to approach a journalist
  4. Why it matters beyond the mention

Businesses tend to think press coverage requires an agency on retainer and a relationship with an editor. It mostly requires understanding that a journalist has a page to fill today and your announcement does not fill it.

Coverage follows from having something genuinely worth reporting and making it effortless to report.

Your news is not news

A new branch, a new managing director, an anniversary, a rebrand. These matter enormously inside the business and not at all outside it. An editor reads them and sees an advertisement that expects to be free.

What does get covered, reliably:

  • Numbers nobody else has. You sit on data about your own market that no journalist can obtain elsewhere.
  • A trend with evidence. Something changing in your sector that you can demonstrate rather than assert.
  • A human story with a named person and a real outcome.
  • Useful expertise, offered when a story is already running and a reporter needs someone who knows the subject.

Nobody owes you coverage for existing. They will take it for something genuinely useful to their readers.

The data angle is the most available one

This is the route most small businesses can actually take. You already have information: what people bought this season versus last, which parts fail most often, how prices have moved, what customers ask about now that they never used to.

Turn it into something a reader would find interesting. Keep it honest, keep the sample size visible, and say plainly how you gathered it. One modest, clearly-sourced finding is worth more than a grand claim nobody can check.

How to approach a journalist

  1. Read what they have written for the past month. Not their outlet. Them.
  2. Email with a subject line that is the story, not your company name.
  3. Three short paragraphs: what you found, why it matters now, what you can provide.
  4. Attach nothing. Offer the data, the photographs and a named person available to speak.
  5. Say when you are available. 'Any time today or tomorrow' is worth more than a glossy pack.

Then let it go. One follow-up after three days is reasonable. A second is not.

3

paragraphs is the whole pitch. Anything longer is read as a press release and deleted.

Why it matters beyond the mention

Coverage is not only about the people who read the article. A credible mention somewhere other than your own website is exactly the corroboration that search engines and AI assistants look for when deciding whether to name you. A single piece in a publication that matters can do more for how you are described online than months of directory listings.

Selling online in Kenya: M-Pesa, delivery and trust

Why carts are abandoned here, and the checkout changes that recover the orders.

Faith MwikaliWeb Design & Development Aug 22, 2026 3 min read Share

On this page

  1. Put mobile money first, visually
  2. Show delivery cost before the last step
  3. Trust is the quiet blocker
  4. Recover the carts you lose anyway
  5. What to fix first

Roughly seven in ten carts are abandoned before payment. That figure is similar worldwide, but the reasons here are specific, and so are the fixes. Most of them are checkout problems rather than product problems, which is good news: they can be fixed without changing anything you sell.

Put mobile money first, visually

A checkout that lists card payment first and mobile money underneath is designed for a different country. Most of your buyers intend to pay by M-Pesa from the moment they add to cart, and every second spent looking for it is a second spent reconsidering.

Make it the first and largest option. If you use a till or paybill, show the number on screen as well as triggering the prompt, because prompts fail and people want a fallback they can see.

Show delivery cost before the last step

This is the single most common reason people leave. A buyer who has mentally committed at 2,400 shillings and discovers 850 shillings of delivery at the final step does not feel informed. They feel caught out, and they leave.

Shown early, the same cost rarely stops the sale. Put delivery costs on the product page, or state a simple rule prominently: free within Nairobi CBD over a threshold, a flat rate elsewhere, a named courier for upcountry.

62%

of abandoned carts in the shops we audit cite unclear or late delivery cost as the reason, ahead of every other cause.

Trust is the quiet blocker

Buying online here still carries a real fear of paying and receiving nothing. Most shops do nothing to address it, then wonder why visitors browse and leave.

  • Real photographs of real stock, taken on a phone in your own premises. Stock images suggest you may not have the item.
  • A physical address and a phone number in the footer and on the checkout page.
  • Reviews with names, even a handful.
  • A plain returns policy in ordinary language, not a legal page.
  • An order confirmation that arrives immediately, by message as well as email.

Buyers here are not looking for a beautiful shop. They are looking for proof the item exists and will arrive.

Recover the carts you lose anyway

Some abandonment is unavoidable. A single reminder, sent within the hour on the channel the buyer already uses, recovers a useful share of it.

Keep it short and specific: the item, the price, the delivery cost, and a direct link back. One message. A sequence of five is an irritation that costs you the customer permanently.

What to fix first

  1. Move mobile money to the top of the checkout and make it visually dominant.
  2. Publish delivery costs on the product page.
  3. Replace every stock photograph with a real one.
  4. Add address, phone and returns policy where they can be seen at checkout.
  5. Set up one abandoned cart reminder, sent within the hour.

In the shops we have done this for, those five changes together typically move completed orders more than any amount of additional advertising spend would have.

  • Mobile money first and largest. Everything else is secondary here.
  • Delivery cost on the product page, never at the final step.
  • Real photographs, real address, real phone number. Trust is the quiet blocker.
  • One reminder within the hour, not a sequence of five.
  • Fix the checkout before buying more traffic.

Branding

Building a brand that works on a matatu and a phone

Most brand guidelines are written for a boardroom. Yours has to survive a moving vehicle and a cracked screen.

Naomi WanjalaHead of Social Media Aug 15, 2026 3 min read Share

On this page

  1. The two tests
  2. Fewer colours, higher contrast
  3. Pick a name people can say and spell
  4. Consistency beats sophistication
  5. Write the one-page version

Most brand design is judged on a large screen, in good light, by people sitting still. Then it goes out into the world and is seen at speed on the side of a vehicle, or at two centimetres across on a phone with a cracked screen in bright sun. A great many brands do not survive the journey.

Designing for those two conditions, rather than for the presentation, produces better work and costs nothing extra.

The two tests

Before approving anything, apply both.

  1. The vehicle test. Print it small, stick it on a wall, walk past at normal speed from four metres. Can you read the name and the phone number? If not, it fails, however elegant it looks on screen.
  2. The thumbnail test. Shrink the logo to 48 pixels, the size of a profile picture. Is it still recognisable, or is it a grey smudge?

Most logos fail the second test because they contain a tagline, a founding date and an illustration with fine lines. Those details are invisible at the size the logo is actually used.

Your logo spends most of its life at the size of a fingernail. Design it for that, not for the letterhead.

Fewer colours, higher contrast

Two colours plus a neutral is enough, and the pair must be strongly different in lightness, not just in hue. Two colours of similar brightness look fine on a calibrated monitor and turn into one colour in sunlight or on a cheap screen.

Test every combination you plan to use as text on a background, and reject anything you cannot read comfortably at arm's length outdoors.

Pick a name people can say and spell

A name gets passed on by word of mouth more here than almost anywhere. If someone cannot spell it after hearing it once, they cannot search for it, and the recommendation is wasted.

  • Short, and pronounceable the same way by everyone who will say it.
  • Spellable after one hearing, without a clarification.
  • Not a near-miss of an established business in your sector.
  • Available as a domain and as a handle on the platforms you will actually use.

Consistency beats sophistication

The brands that feel established in this market are rarely the most sophisticated ones. They are the ones that look identical everywhere: the same name, the same colour, the same typeface on the shopfront, the receipt, the Facebook page, the delivery vehicle and the invoice.

A simple identity applied consistently reads as a serious business. A sophisticated identity applied inconsistently reads as several small ones.

48px

the size at which your logo does most of its work. If it is not legible there, it is not finished.

Write the one-page version

Full brand guideline documents are rarely opened. Produce one page that anyone can follow: the logo files and the minimum size, the two colour values, the typeface and where to get it, and three sentences on how the business talks. Give it to the signwriter, the printer and whoever runs the social accounts.

That one page, actually used, will do more than fifty pages that live in a folder.

Send us your logo and a photograph of your shopfront. We will tell you what is working and what disappears at small sizes.

What to take away

  • The vehicle test and the thumbnail test decide whether an identity works here.
  • Two colours plus a neutral, strongly different in lightness.
  • A name people can spell after hearing it once.
  • Consistency reads as established. Sophistication applied unevenly does not.
  • One page of guidelines that people actually use.
